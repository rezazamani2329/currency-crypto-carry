\begin{abstract}
\noindent
Do carry trades crash when they are crowded? We study G10 currency carry (1999--2026) and crypto funding-rate carry on Binance perpetual futures (2020--2026). FX carry earns a net Sharpe ratio of 0.37 and loses when volatility rises. Crowding measured from CFTC speculator positions does not predict FX carry returns or crashes in portfolio, panel, or tail tests. Crypto carry collects funding income, has no directional exposure to Bitcoin, and crashes when single coins with extreme funding collapse. The two strategies are nearly uncorrelated. Crowding tells investors where carry risk sits, not when it will materialize.
\end{abstract}

\vspace{1em}

\section{Introduction}
%==========================================================

Uncovered interest parity (UIP) says that a currency with a high interest rate should depreciate by exactly the interest differential, so that borrowing in a low-rate currency and lending in a high-rate one earns nothing on average. Since \citet{HansenHodrick1980} and \citet{Fama1984}, a large literature has shown the opposite: high-yield currencies tend to appreciate, or depreciate by less than the rate gap, and the carry trade earns a sizeable premium. The leading explanation is that this premium compensates for crash risk. Carry trades go ``up the stairs and down the elevator'': they earn small steady gains most of the time and lose a large amount in a few weeks when global risk appetite falls \citep{BrunnermeierNagelPedersen2008, Menkhoff2012, Farhi2016}.

\citet{BrunnermeierNagelPedersen2008} add a second ingredient. Crashes are worse when speculators hold large positions in the trade, because a funding shock forces them to unwind together. Crowding is therefore a natural candidate for predicting \emph{when} carry will crash. This paper asks a simple question: \emph{do carry trades crash when they are crowded?}

We study the question in two markets that share the economics of carry but differ in who trades. The first is the classic G10 currency market, where carry is the gap between short-term interest rates and crowding can be measured from the Commodity Futures Trading Commission (CFTC) report on speculators' futures positions. The second is the market for crypto perpetual futures. A perpetual future has no expiry; instead, longs and shorts exchange a \emph{funding rate} every eight hours that keeps the futures price close to spot. When leveraged traders crowd into long positions, longs pay shorts. The funding rate is therefore both the crypto analogue of an interest-rate differential and a direct measure of crowded leveraged demand.

We build two pre-specified, dollar-neutral strategies. Strategy~A is a monthly G10 carry strategy that buys the two currencies with the highest three-month interest rates and sells the two with the lowest, and halves its positions when CFTC positioning shows that its longs are crowded long and its shorts crowded short. Strategy~B is a weekly crypto carry strategy that shorts the third of coins with the highest seven-day funding and buys the third with the lowest. All parameters, the in-sample/out-of-sample split, and transaction costs were fixed before any backtest was run.

We find four main results. First, G10 carry earns a modest premium after costs (Sharpe ratio 0.37 over 1999--2026) and closely tracks the academic carry factor of \citet{LRV2011}, with a correlation of 0.82. Its losses line up with volatility shocks: in a regression on the dollar factor and equity-volatility innovations, the volatility beta is $-1.55$ ($t=-3.7$) and the alpha is not significant. This is the standard risk-based picture of carry.

Second, CFTC crowding does not predict FX carry. Carry earns 0.02\% in the month after a crowded reading against 0.30\% otherwise, but the difference is not significant ($t=-0.57$), and it shrinks once we drop the years in which the crowding signal is unavailable. Stronger tests give the same answer. In a panel of 2,149 currency-months, a currency's own crowding z-score does not predict its next-month excess return ($t=0.45$); the portfolio crowding level does not predict carry returns at horizons of one to twelve months; and crowded months are not more likely to be crash months. The 2008 crash started from a \emph{negative} crowding reading. A filter that halves exposure when crowded raises the out-of-sample Sharpe ratio from 0.27 to 0.32 under every parameter setting we try, but this gain is not statistically significant ($t=1.0$).

Third, crypto funding carry collects real funding income of about 8.5\% a year, but price moves decide its performance. Net of costs it earns 4.1\% a year with 30\% volatility (Sharpe 0.14), losing in 2020--2023 and earning a Sharpe of 0.81 since 2024. The strategy has no directional Bitcoin exposure: the beta is precisely estimated near zero (daily $-0.02$, 95\% confidence interval $[-0.06, 0.03]$) at daily, weekly, and monthly horizons, with no downside asymmetry, and its crashes are coin-specific. They arise in the mirror image of FX: because the strategy buys coins with the most negative funding, it ends up long whatever traders short hardest. In May 2022 that meant being long Terra (LUNA) into its collapse, which cost 27\% in a single week.

Fourth, FX and crypto carry are almost uncorrelated (0.11). A 50/50 mix scaled to equal volatility has lower volatility and a smaller drawdown than either strategy alone.

Our answer to the main question therefore differs across markets. In FX, the answer is no: carry crashes are compensation for exposure to global volatility, and CFTC positioning adds no predictive power. In crypto, crowding measured by funding tells an investor \emph{where} the danger is, in the coins with extreme funding on either side, but not \emph{when} it will hit.

\paragraph{Related literature.}
Our paper connects three strands of work. The first documents the carry premium and explains it with risk. \citet{LustigVerdelhan2007} and \citet{LRV2011} show that a high-minus-low carry factor prices the cross-section of currency portfolios, and \citet{LRV2014} show that currency risk premia are countercyclical. \citet{Menkhoff2012} identify global FX volatility as the priced risk, \citet{Burnside2011} discuss peso problems, \citet{Jurek2014} studies crash-hedged carry, \citet{DanielHodrickLu2017} document carry drawdowns, and \citet{Farhi2016} model rare disasters. \citet{Koijen2018} show that carry predicts returns in many asset classes. We replicate the standard G10 result and its link to volatility risk.

The second strand studies crowding and forced unwinding. \citet{BrunnermeierPedersen2009} model the spiral between market liquidity and funding liquidity, \citet{Pedersen2009} describes how crowded trades crash when ``everyone runs for the exit,'' and \citet{Stein2009} discusses how crowding by sophisticated investors can destabilize prices. \citet{BrunnermeierNagelPedersen2008} show that speculator positions in currency futures predict the skewness of carry returns, and \citet{Moskowitz2012} use the same CFTC data to study how speculators trade on trends. Other studies measure crowding without futures data: \citet{PojarlievLevich2011} infer it from the returns of currency funds and find that carry was crowded just before the 2008 unwind, and \citet{Sokolovski2016} measures it from co-movement among carry currencies and hedge fund exposures and finds that high crowding doubles the probability of an extreme carry loss.

The third strand studies cryptocurrency returns. \citet{LiuTsyvinski2021} and \citet{LiuTsyvinskiWu2022} document the risk factors that explain crypto returns, \citet{He2022} describe the pricing of perpetual futures, and \citet{Schmeling2023} show that crypto carry, the futures--spot basis, reflects leveraged demand from retail investors and predicts crash risk. \citet{Christin2023} document the high Sharpe ratio of the Bitcoin funding trade, its link to the leverage that exchanges allow, and its losses in the Terra and FTX collapses. These papers study the \emph{time-series} carry trade, long spot and short the perpetual of the same coin. 
\paragraph{Recent evidence (2024--2026).} A new wave of work, much of it prompted by the August 2024 unwind of the yen carry trade, revisits both strands. On the FX side, \citet{Aquilina2024} show that speculative short yen positions in CFTC data had reached record highs before the unwind, and \citet{BIS2024} document that hedge funds' exposure to carry had risen steadily since 2022 across many strategy types. These studies describe the build-up of crowding in one episode, in the spirit of \citet{BrunnermeierNagelPedersen2008}, but do not test whether positioning predicts crashes systematically. \citet{CheemaFox2025} measure positioning from institutional investor flows instead of futures data and show that crises differ: some are flights to safety into the dollar, while August 2024 was a flight from carry centered on the yen, and flows around the April 2025 tariff announcement were driven more by carry than by safe-haven demand. \citet{Kutuk2025} use option-implied crash measures for dollar--lira carry and find that 46--77\% of carry returns compensate for crash exposure, extending the risk-based view of \citet{Menkhoff2012} and \citet{Farhi2016} to forward-looking measures. Together these papers suggest that crowding matters, but they measure it with flows, fund exposures, or option prices rather than CFTC positions, which is the gap our FX tests address.

On the crypto side, \citet{Schmeling2025} update the evidence of \citet{Schmeling2023}: the basis reflects an ``inconvenience yield'' of holding spot, driven by trend-chasing, leveraged retail demand and by arbitrage capital that is scarce because of margin and regulatory limits. \citet{Borri2025} show that the Bitcoin funding trade studied by \citet{Christin2023} earned a Sharpe ratio above six over 2020--2025, but that its premium compressed sharply after 2024 and turned negative in 2025. \citet{Gornall2025} show that perpetual futures reduce extreme price dislocations and the drawdowns of arbitrage strategies relative to dated futures. All three study the time-series trade in one coin at a time, mostly Bitcoin. None studies the cross-section of coins sorted on funding, the setting that corresponds most closely to the FX carry trade, and none compares crypto carry with FX carry. Our paper fills these gaps. Table~\ref{tab:lit} summarizes these studies, their methods, and their findings, and how each relates to our results.

\paragraph{Contributions.} Relative to this literature, the paper makes four contributions.

\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{A comprehensive test of CFTC crowding as a predictor of carry returns and crashes.} \citet{BrunnermeierNagelPedersen2008} show that speculator positions predict the \emph{skewness} of carry returns. We ask whether they predict carry \emph{returns} and \emph{crashes}, and whether an investor could use them in real time. We test this with a pre-specified trading rule evaluated out of sample through 2026, a currency-level panel of 2,149 observations, predictive regressions at horizons of one to twelve months, and tests on the left tail of returns. All give the same answer: CFTC crowding does not predict FX carry. Read together with \citet{PojarlievLevich2011}, \citet{Sokolovski2016}, and \citet{CheemaFox2025}, who find that crowding matters when it is measured from fund returns, currency co-movement, or investor flows, our evidence suggests that weekly futures positions are too slow and too partial a proxy for the crowding that drives carry crashes.

\item \emph{A cross-sectional crypto carry strategy built on funding rates.} Existing studies of crypto carry \citep{Christin2023, Schmeling2025, Borri2025} examine the time-series trade that holds a coin and shorts its perpetual. To our knowledge, we are the first to study the cross-sectional analogue of FX carry: long the coins with the lowest funding and short those with the highest, on a universe that keeps the coins that collapsed (Terra and FTX Token) to avoid survivorship bias. We decompose its return into funding and price components and show that funding income is real but price moves dominate.

\item \emph{Evidence that crypto carry risk is coin-specific, not market-wide.} We show that the cross-sectional strategy has no directional Bitcoin exposure, with a beta precisely estimated near zero at every horizon and no downside asymmetry, and that its crashes come from a few coins at the extremes of the funding distribution. The worst losses arise from being long coins that traders were crowding to short (Terra and FTX Token in 2022) and short coins that retail traders were crowding to buy (Dogecoin and XRP in 2021). This links the leveraged-demand mechanism of \citet{Schmeling2025} to the cross-section of coins.

\item \emph{A side-by-side comparison of FX and crypto carry under one design.} Using the same pre-specified framework in both markets, we show two different channels of crowding risk. In FX, crashes are driven by global volatility shocks that hit carry regardless of positioning. In crypto, the funding rate is both the carry signal and the crowding signal, and crowding determines \emph{where} losses occur. Because the two strategies are nearly uncorrelated (0.11), combining them lowers volatility and drawdowns. All code, data, and results are public, so every table and figure can be reproduced.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Hypotheses}\label{sec:hyp}
%==========================================================

We split the main question into six research questions: (RQ1) whether G10 carry earns a premium after costs and reproduces the academic carry factor; (RQ2) whether crowding predicts weaker or more crash-prone carry; (RQ3) when FX carry loses money; (RQ4) whether crypto funding carry earns a premium and whether it comes from funding or from price moves; (RQ5) what drives crypto carry losses; and (RQ6) whether FX and crypto carry diversify each other. The corresponding hypotheses, written down before any backtest was run, are:

\begin{description}\setlength{\itemsep}{0.2em}
\item[H-A1.] G10 carry earns a positive expected excess return after transaction costs. The null is a zero expected return.
\item[H-A2.] Carry is weaker and more negatively skewed in the month after a crowded reading, so that cutting exposure when crowded raises the out-of-sample Sharpe ratio. The null is that next-month carry is the same after crowded and non-crowded months.
\item[H-A3.] Carry loses when volatility rises. The null is that carry returns are unrelated to volatility shocks.
\item[H-B1.] Shorting high-funding coins and buying low-funding coins earns a positive return after costs, mainly from the funding it collects.
\item[H-B2.] Because it is dollar-neutral, crypto carry has little exposure to Bitcoin, and its crashes are coin-specific rather than market-wide.
\item[H-C.] FX carry and crypto carry have low correlation, so a combined portfolio diversifies.
\end{description}

\section{Methodology}\label{sec:method}
%==========================================================

This section describes how we build the two strategies and test the hypotheses of Section~\ref{sec:hyp}. Both strategies are pre-specified and dollar-neutral, with all parameters fixed before any backtest was run. Sections~\ref{sec:methA} and \ref{sec:methB} define Strategy~A, the G10 carry strategy with the CFTC crowding filter, and Strategy~B, the cross-sectional crypto funding carry strategy. Section~\ref{sec:methcost} sets out transaction costs and performance measures, Section~\ref{sec:methrisk} the risk regressions, and Section~\ref{sec:methcomb} the combined portfolio. Section~\ref{sec:methhonest} explains the steps we take to limit data mining.

\subsection{Strategy A: Crowded G10 Carry}\label{sec:methA}

Let $S_{k,t}$ be the spot rate of currency $k$ in US dollars per unit and $i_{k,t}$ its three-month interest rate in percent per year. The monthly excess return of a long position in currency $k$ is
\begin{equation}
rx_{k,t} = \left(1+\frac{i_{k,t-1}}{1200}\right)\frac{S_{k,t}}{S_{k,t-1}} - \left(1+\frac{i_{US,t-1}}{1200}\right),
\end{equation}
which is the return on a one-month forward position under covered interest parity.

At each month-end $t$ we rank the seven currencies by the interest differential $i_{k,t}-i_{US,t}$ and assign weight $w_{k,t}=+0.5$ to each of the two highest and $w_{k,t}=-0.5$ to each of the two lowest. The portfolio has no net dollar exposure, and its return in month $t+1$ is $r_{t+1}=\sum_k w_{k,t}\,rx_{k,t+1}$.

\paragraph{Crowding signal.} For each currency we compute speculators' net position scaled by open interest from the last CFTC report available at month-end,
\begin{equation}
pos_{k,t} = \frac{\text{non-commercial long}_{k,t} - \text{non-commercial short}_{k,t}}{\text{open interest}_{k,t}},
\end{equation}
and standardize it over a rolling 36-month window (minimum 24 months): $z_{k,t} = (pos_{k,t}-\bar{pos}_{k})/\sigma_{k}$. The CFTC report records Tuesday positions and is published on Friday, so we use each report only from its release date. Portfolio crowding is
\begin{equation}
\text{crowd}_t = \sum_k w_{k,t}\, z_{k,t},
\end{equation}
the average z-score of the currencies we buy minus the average z-score of those we sell. A high value means speculators are already long our longs and short our shorts. The crowd-filtered strategy multiplies all weights by 0.5 when $\text{crowd}_t>1$.

Figure~\ref{fig:pos} shows how stable the portfolio is. In practice it is long the New Zealand dollar in 94\% of months and the Australian dollar in 75\%, and short the Swiss franc in 99\% of months, often together with the yen (52\%) or the euro (40\%). The Canadian dollar and the pound move between the legs as their rates change relative to the others. Thirty-three of the 328 months are classified as crowded.

\subsection{Strategy B: Crypto Funding Carry}\label{sec:methB}

Let $f_{c,t}$ be the average daily funding rate of coin $c$ over the seven days up to day $t-1$. A coin is tradable on a day only if it has a price and positive volume, which removes LUNA after May 2022 and FTT after November 14, 2022. Every Sunday we assign weight $-0.5/k$ to the $k$ coins with the highest $f_{c,t}$ and $+0.5/k$ to the $k$ coins with the lowest, where $k$ is one third of the tradable coins. Each leg usually holds three coins (2,151 of 2,403 days); it holds two in early 2020 and briefly four in 2022. Positions are held for seven days, with daily profit and loss
\begin{equation}
\text{pnl}_{c,t+1} = w_{c,t}\,\frac{P_{c,t+1}-P_{c,t}}{P_{c,t}} - w_{c,t}\,\text{funding}_{c,t+1}.
\end{equation}
A short position ($w<0$) therefore receives funding when funding is positive. We decompose net returns into a funding component, a price component, and costs. There is no separate crowding filter in Strategy~B, because the funding rate is itself the crowding signal.

\subsection{Transaction Costs and Performance Measures}\label{sec:methcost}

Costs are proportional to turnover, $\text{cost}_t = c\sum_k |w_{k,t}-w_{k,t-1}|$, with $c=3$ basis points for G10 currencies and $c=5$ basis points for crypto perpetuals. We report annualized mean, volatility, and Sharpe ratio (scaled by 12 for monthly and 365 for daily data), maximum drawdown, skewness, worst month or week, hit rate, and annual turnover. Because the strategies are dollar-neutral and earn excess returns, the Sharpe ratio is the mean divided by volatility.

\subsection{Risk Regressions}\label{sec:methrisk}

We estimate the following regressions with \citet{NeweyWest1987} standard errors (3 lags for monthly data, 7 lags for daily data):
\begin{align}
r^{A}_{t} &= \alpha + \beta_{RX}\, RX_t + \beta_{vol}\,\Delta Vol_t + \varepsilon_t, \qquad \text{monthly, 1999:05--2021:05},\\
r^{B}_{t} &= \alpha + \beta_{BTC}\, r^{BTC}_t + \varepsilon_t, \qquad \text{daily, 2020--2026},
\end{align}
where $RX_t$ is the dollar factor and $\Delta Vol_t$ the equity-volatility innovation from \citet{LRV2011}. The factor data end in May 2021, which limits the sample of the FX regression.

\subsection{Combined Portfolio}\label{sec:methcomb}

To compare the strategies on equal terms, we scale each to 10\% annual volatility using trailing volatility known at $t-1$ (36 months for A, 12 months for B) and combine them 50/50.

\subsection{Keeping the Tests Honest}\label{sec:methhonest}

Several features of the design limit data mining. All parameters (number of currencies per leg, z-score window, crowding threshold, exposure cut, funding lookback, rebalancing frequency, and costs) were fixed before the backtests and were not changed afterwards. Results are split into an in-sample period (1999--2010 for A, 2020--2023 for B) and an out-of-sample period (2011--2026 for A, 2024--2026 for B). Weights set at the end of period $t$ earn the return of $t+1$; CFTC data are used only after their release; and crypto signals use funding up to the previous day. Robustness grids are reported in full but were not used to choose the headline parameters.

\subsubsection{Does Crowding Predict Weak Carry? (H-A2)}

Table~\ref{tab:crowd} compares carry in the month after crowded and non-crowded readings. After the 294 non-crowded months, carry earns 0.30\% in the next month on average. After the 33 crowded months it earns only 0.02\%, with similar volatility and more negative skewness ($-0.71$ against $-0.46$). The difference is economically meaningful but not statistically significant: a Welch test of equal means gives $t=-0.57$ ($p=0.58$). Part of the gap also reflects timing. Comparing only months in which the measure is available, carry earns 0.22\% after non-crowded readings against 0.02\% after crowded ones ($t=-0.39$).

\subsubsection{What Drives Crypto Carry Losses? (H-B2)}

Figure~\ref{fig:cdd} plots the drawdown of Strategy~B. The drawdown builds up in 2021 and 2022, during the Dogecoin and XRP rally and the Terra and FTX collapses, and reaches $-67$\% on November 9, 2022. The strategy has not regained its 2021 peak: despite the gains since 2024, it ends August 2026 about 55\% below it. Crypto carry has no directional market exposure. A daily regression on Bitcoin returns gives a beta of $-0.02$ (Newey--West $t=-0.75$) and an $R^2$ of 0.001. Because the claim here is that the beta is zero, the relevant statistic is the width of its confidence interval rather than the $t$-statistic. The 95\% interval is $[-0.06, 0.03]$, which rules out even a small exposure. Table~\ref{tab:cbeta} shows that the result holds at weekly and monthly horizons, when lagged Bitcoin returns are added to allow for slow price adjustment, and on days when Bitcoin falls. In the 5\% of days on which Bitcoin fell most (by 7.2\% on average), the strategy earned $+0.15$\%, and in Bitcoin's worst 10\% of weeks it lost an insignificant 0.6\%. Exposure is not constant, however: the rolling one-year beta ranges from $-0.21$ to $+0.19$, and the size of daily strategy returns is correlated with the size of Bitcoin's moves (0.20), so the strategy is more volatile in turbulent markets even though it has no directional exposure. Its losses are instead coin-specific. The worst week, ending May 15, 2022, cost 27.3\%. In that week the strategy was \emph{long} Terra: traders were shorting LUNA so heavily that its seven-day funding had turned negative, about $-20$\% a year, and the rule placed it in the long leg just before it collapsed. The other worst weeks ended September 12, 2021 ($-12.3$\%), November 13, 2022 ($-11.6$\%, the FTX collapse), November 29, 2020 ($-11.0$\%), and August 15, 2021 ($-10.0$\%).

This pattern is the mirror image of FX carry. In FX, carry crashes when a crowded long position in high-yield currencies unwinds. The evidence supports H-B2: crypto carry is market-neutral, but its crashes come from single coins with extreme funding.

We test whether the main results depend on parameter choices made before the backtests were run. Figure~\ref{fig:rob} collects the two parameter grids of Tables~\ref{tab:Arob} and~\ref{tab:Crob}. \paragraph{Interpretation.}
In foreign exchange, our results agree with the risk-based view of carry. G10 carry earns a modest premium, tracks the academic carry factor, and loses when volatility rises. Crowding measured from CFTC positions, however, does not predict carry returns or crashes. The point estimates go in the expected direction in the simplest comparison, but none of our tests is significant, and the largest crash in our sample started from a position that did not look crowded. The 2008 unwind was driven by a global funding shock that hit carry whether or not speculators were heavily positioned, consistent with \citet{BrunnermeierPedersen2009}. Our null result should be read alongside studies that do find a role for crowding. \citet{Sokolovski2016} and \citet{PojarlievLevich2011} measure crowding from currency co-movement and fund returns, \citet{CheemaFox2025} from institutional flows, and \citet{Kutuk2025} price crash risk from options. These measures are faster and capture investors that the CFTC futures report misses, such as banks' forward books, real-money investors, and Japanese retail margin traders. Individual episodes show both sides. Our crowding measure was high before the August 2007 loss ($-7.8$\%) and before the July 2024 loss ($-4.7$\%) that began the yen unwind documented by \citet{Aquilina2024}, and in both cases the filter halved exposure. But high readings also preceded many ordinary months, and the 2008 crash began from a negative reading. Positioning in futures may therefore identify fragile episodes after the fact without being a reliable systematic signal of when crowding will unwind.

In crypto, the funding rate is a direct and fast measure of crowded leveraged demand. Sorting on it collects real income, but it also places the strategy against the crowd on both sides. Being short crowded longs lost money in the 2021 retail rally, and being long crowded shorts lost money in the 2022 collapses. This is consistent with \citet{Schmeling2025}, who find that high funding reflects leveraged retail demand meeting scarce arbitrage capital, and with \citet{Christin2023}, who find that the funding trade suffered most in the Terra and FTX collapses. Our cross-sectional design adds that the losses come from the coins at the extremes of the funding distribution. The fall in funding income after 2024 (from 12.3\% to 3.1\% a year) matches the compression of the Bitcoin funding premium documented by \citet{Borri2025}. Crowding therefore tells an investor \emph{where} the risk sits rather than \emph{when} it will materialize.

\paragraph{Implications.}
For FX investors, CFTC positioning data are not a reliable timing signal; the small out-of-sample gain from the filter is not significant, and protection against volatility shocks matters more. For crypto, sorting on funding alone exposes a portfolio to single-coin collapses. A better design would react faster (the robustness grid points towards shorter lookbacks and daily rebalancing), treat extremely negative funding as a warning of crowded shorts rather than an opportunity, and size positions by coin-level risk. In FX, a rule that reduces exposure when either crowding or implied volatility is high may protect against both kinds of crash, and adding emerging-market currencies would raise the number of crowded episodes and test whether crowding matters more where liquidity is thinner. In crypto, extreme negative funding should be tested as a warning signal in a larger universe that includes coins that later collapsed, and a practical design would also account for slippage, funding caps, and the risk of exchange failure. The combined portfolio could add carry in other asset classes \citep{Koijen2018}, although the premium in the simplest crypto carry trade has compressed since 2024 \citep{Borri2025}.

\paragraph{Limitations.}
Our results have several limitations. The crypto sample covers about six and a half years and twelve coins, each leg usually holds three coins, and single-coin events dominate. Strategy~B reverses between the in-sample period (Sharpe $-0.07$) and the out-of-sample period (0.81), and the out-of-sample gains came mostly from price moves, so they are not evidence of a stable premium. In FX, only 33 months were crowded, which limits the power of the simple comparison, although the panel and continuous tests use the full sample, and the crowding measure is missing from mid-1999 to mid-2006, so the filter is tested only on the last twenty years. Data availability limits the FX sample to start in 1999 and the factor regressions to end in 2021. Finally, transaction costs are proportional estimates; we do not model funding caps, borrowing limits, slippage under stress, or exchange counterparty risk, which was material in the FTX collapse.

%==========================================================
\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:concl}
%==========================================================

We ask whether carry trades crash when they are crowded, using G10 currencies and crypto perpetual futures. Our answer differs across the two markets. In FX, carry crashes are compensation for exposure to volatility risk, and crowding measured from CFTC positions does not predict carry returns or crashes in any of our tests. In crypto, carry also earns a premium and also crashes, but the crashes come from single coins, mostly from being long a coin that traders are crowding to short, so crowding tells investors where the danger is rather than when it will hit. Because the two carry trades are nearly uncorrelated, combining them reduces volatility and drawdowns.

Our results point to three directions for future work. First, the crowding that drives FX carry crashes may be better captured by faster measures than weekly futures positions, such as option-implied crash risk \citep{Kutuk2025}, institutional flows \citep{CheemaFox2025}, or co-movement among carry currencies \citep{Sokolovski2016}, possibly combined with volatility. Second, in crypto, open interest, liquidation data, and funding across exchanges could sharpen the funding signal, and the delta-neutral basis trade \citep{Christin2023, Gornall2025} offers funding income without coin-specific price risk. Third, coin-level risk limits and estimated weights in the combined portfolio would bring both strategies closer to what an investor could trade.
