Crowded Carry: crash risk in FX and crypto carry trades

A complete study guide to the project: the economics behind it, every step of the process from raw data to results, worked numerical examples, the full results, and what to say when the professor asks.

00Summary in one minute

A carry trade borrows in a low-yield asset and invests in a high-yield one. It pays a small premium most of the time and occasionally crashes. The leading explanation is that the premium pays you for crash risk, and that crashes are worst when the trade is crowded: when many speculators hold it and must all unwind at once.

We built two carry strategies with the same logic in two very different markets and asked whether crowding helps predict the crashes:

  • A · FX Crowded G10 carry. Long the 2 highest-rate developed currencies, short the 2 lowest, monthly. Crowding is measured from CFTC futures positioning. When the trade is crowded, we cut exposure in half.
  • C · crypto Crypto funding carry. On Binance perpetual futures, short the third of coins with the highest funding rate and long the third with the lowest, rebalanced weekly.

The one-sentence story. Carry in both markets behaves like a reward for crash risk; in FX, crowding is a modest but useful warning sign; in crypto, the strategy's crashes come from single coins (LUNA, FTT), and because FX and crypto carry crash at different times, they diversify each other.

01The big idea

Why should carry make money at all?

Theory says it should not. Uncovered interest parity (UIP) says a high-rate currency should depreciate by exactly the interest differential, so borrowing yen at 0.1% to hold Australian dollars at 4% earns nothing on average. In the data, UIP fails: high-rate currencies do not depreciate enough on average, and often appreciate. This is the forward premium puzzle, and it is why carry earns money.

Then why doesn't everyone do it?

Because the profits come with negative skewness: many small gains and a few large, sudden losses. The saying is that carry goes "up the stairs and down the elevator". Losses cluster in bad times for everyone (2008, sudden volatility spikes), so the premium can be read as compensation for risk, not a free lunch.

Where does crowding come in?

Brunnermeier, Nagel and Pedersen (2008) show that carry crashes happen when speculators are heavily positioned in the trade and funding conditions tighten. If everyone is long AUD and short JPY, a shock forces them to sell AUD and buy JPY at the same time, and their own selling deepens the crash. So: a crowded carry trade should have more crash risk. That is the hypothesis we test in FX, using CFTC positioning as the crowding gauge.

Why crypto?

Crypto perpetual futures have their own interest rate: the funding rate. When many traders are leveraged long a coin, they pay a high funding rate to the shorts. So a high funding rate is both a carry signal (shorts get paid) and a crowding signal (lots of leveraged longs). Same economics, completely different investors and market structure. It lets us ask whether the carry and crash logic carries over.

An analogy

Carry is like selling insurance. You collect a premium every month and pay out rarely, but when you pay, it is large and comes at the worst time. Crowding is like many insurers having written the same policy: when disaster comes, they all try to offload risk at once.

The two markets side by side

FX yield = interest differential, crowding = CFTC positions.
Crypto yield = funding rate, and funding itself measures leveraged demand.

02Concepts you need

Grouped from the foundations up. Each card gives the definition and how this project uses it.

Currency foundations

Spot rate S

Price of one unit of foreign currency in USD. We store all rates as USD per foreign unit, so S going up means the foreign currency appreciated.

FRED series like DEXUSAL are already USD per AUD; DEXJPUS (yen per USD) is inverted.

Interest differential

Foreign 3-month rate minus the US 3-month rate, ik − iUS. This is the carry signal in Strategy A.

Data: OECD 3-month interbank rates via FRED (IR3TIB01…M156N).

Covered interest parity (CIP)

Forward = Spot × (1 + iUS) / (1 + ik). It means buying a currency forward is the same as borrowing dollars and depositing in the foreign currency. That is why we can compute carry returns from spot and rates without forward data.

Uncovered interest parity (UIP)

Expected depreciation equals the interest differential. If it held, carry would earn zero. Its empirical failure is the forward premium puzzle.

Currency excess return rx

The return of borrowing USD and holding foreign currency for one month: foreign interest plus currency move, minus US interest.

Formula and worked example in section 5.

Dollar-neutral portfolio

Long and short legs have equal size, so the portfolio has no net bet on the dollar. It bets only on high-rate currencies beating low-rate ones.

Crowding and positioning

CFTC Commitments of Traders

Weekly US regulator report of positions in futures. For each currency future at the CME it splits traders into commercial (hedgers) and non-commercial (speculators).

Positions as of Tuesday, published Friday. We use them only after Friday.

Net speculative positioning

(Non-commercial long − non-commercial short) / open interest. Positive means speculators are net long the foreign currency.

z-score

(today's value − rolling mean) / rolling standard deviation over 36 months. It says how unusual today's positioning is compared with recent history.

Portfolio crowding

Average z of the long leg minus average z of the short leg. High means speculators are unusually long what we are long and short what we are short. Above 1 we call the trade crowded.

Crypto mechanics

Perpetual future (perp)

A futures contract with no expiry. To keep its price close to spot, longs and shorts exchange a periodic payment called funding.

Funding rate

Paid every 8 hours on Binance. If positive, longs pay shorts; if negative, shorts pay longs. Typical value 0.01% per 8 hours, about 11% a year.

Our data: average funding about 12–15% a year on major coins, positive on 81–88% of days.

Funding as carry

Being short a high-funding coin earns funding, like being long a high-rate currency earns interest. Being long a negative-funding coin also earns funding.

Survivorship bias

Testing only on coins that still exist today makes results look better than they were. We keep LUNA and FTT, which collapsed in 2022.

Statistics and backtesting

Sharpe ratio

Annualised mean excess return divided by annualised volatility. Monthly: mean × 12 / (sd × √12). Daily crypto: × 365.

Maximum drawdown

Largest fall from a previous peak in cumulative value. Captures crash risk that volatility misses.

Skewness

Asymmetry of returns. Negative skew means occasional large losses. FX carry is negatively skewed (−0.49).

Turnover and costs

Turnover = sum of absolute weight changes. Cost = turnover × cost per unit: 3 bp for FX, 5 bp for crypto.

In-sample / out-of-sample

Results on data before the split (A: to 2010, C: to 2023) versus data after it. Out-of-sample is the fairer test.

Look-ahead bias

Using information not yet known at decision time. We avoid it by forming weights at the end of period t and earning period t+1's return, and by lagging CFTC data to its release date.

Newey-West t-statistic

A t-statistic that corrects standard errors for autocorrelation and heteroskedasticity in time-series regressions. We use 3 lags (monthly) and 7 lags (daily).

Volatility targeting

Scale a strategy so its predicted volatility equals a target (10% a year), using only past volatility. Makes two strategies comparable before combining them.

05Strategy A, step by step FX

Universe: JPY, EUR, GBP, CHF, CAD, AUD, NZD, each against USD. Frequency: monthly. Sample: 1999-05 to 2026-08 (limited by the start of JPY and CHF rate data and the euro).

Step 1. Compute each currency's monthly excess return

rx(k, t) = (1 + i_k,t−1 / 1200) × S_t / S_t−1 − (1 + i_US,t−1 / 1200) i in % per year, so /1200 turns it into a monthly decimal. S in USD per foreign unit.
Worked example
a
AUD rate 4.0%, US rate 1.0%. AUD/USD moves from 0.7000 to 0.7035 (+0.5%).
b
rx = (1 + 0.04/12) × 1.005 − (1 + 0.01/12) = 1.003333 × 1.005 − 1.000833 = 1.008350 − 1.000833 = +0.75%.
c
If AUD instead falls 1%: 1.003333 × 0.99 − 1.000833 = −0.75%.
d
Break-even: AUD can fall about 0.25% a month (3% a year) before carry loses. UIP says it should fall exactly that much on average; in the data it doesn't, which is the carry premium.

Step 2. Rank and form the portfolio

At each month-end, rank the 7 currencies by ik − iUS. Go long the top 2 and short the bottom 2. Each long has weight +0.5, each short −0.5 (dollar-neutral).

Worked example (illustrative rates)
Currency3m rateDiff vs USD (3.5%)RankWeight
NZD5.5%+2.01+0.5
AUD4.3%+0.82+0.5
GBP4.0%+0.530
CAD3.0%−0.540

Next month's portfolio return = 0.5 × (rxNZD + rxAUD) − 0.5 × (rxCHF + rxJPY).

Step 3. Measure crowding with CFTC data

  1. For each currency future: net speculative position = (non-commercial long − short) / open interest.
  2. Shift each Tuesday report by 3 days so it is used only after its Friday release; take the last value in each month.
  3. z-score each currency over a rolling 36-month window (at least 24 months needed).
  4. Portfolio crowding = Σ wk × zk = average z of the longs − average z of the shorts.
Worked example
a
z-scores: NZD +1.8, AUD +1.4 (speculators unusually long), CHF −0.6, JPY −1.2 (unusually short).
b
Crowding = 0.5(1.8) + 0.5(1.4) − 0.5(−0.6) − 0.5(−1.2) = 0.9 + 0.7 + 0.3 + 0.6 = 2.5.
c
2.5 > 1, so the trade is crowded: all weights are multiplied by 0.5 for next month.

Step 4. Timing and costs

Weights decided at the end of month t earn month t+1's return. Turnover = Σ |wt − wt−1|, and the cost is turnover × 3 bp.

Example: GBP replaces AUD in the long leg. AUD goes 0.5 → 0 and GBP 0 → 0.5, so turnover = 1.0 and the cost is 0.03% that month. Halving exposure when crowded also creates turnover, which is why the filtered strategy's turnover is higher (2.0× vs 1.2× a year).

06Strategy C, step by step crypto

Universe: 12 Binance USDT-margined perpetuals: BTC, ETH, BNB, XRP, ADA, DOGE, SOL, LTC, LINK, AVAX, plus LUNA and FTT, which collapsed in 2022. Frequency: weekly rebalance, daily P&L. Sample: 2020-02 to 2026-08.

Step 1. Build the signal

Sum each day's three 8-hour funding payments into daily funding. Signal = trailing 7-day average of daily funding, computed at the end of day t and used for the positions held on day t+1.

Step 2. Sort into thirds every Sunday

Among coins that are tradable that day (price and positive volume), with at least 6 coins available: k = number of coins / 3. Short the k coins with the highest funding (weight −0.5/k each), long the k with the lowest (+0.5/k each). Each leg is 50% of capital, so the portfolio is dollar-neutral with gross exposure 1.

Step 3. Daily P&L = price + funding

price P&L = Σ w_k,t−1 × return_k,t funding P&L = − Σ w_k,t−1 × funding_k,t (a short, w < 0, receives positive funding) net = price + funding − turnover × 5 bp
Worked example: one day short DOGE
a
11 coins tradable, so k = 3 and each short has weight −0.5/3 = −16.7%.
b
DOGE funding today: 0.04% (about 15% a year). Funding P&L = −(−0.167) × 0.04% = +0.0067%.
c
DOGE price rises 2% today. Price P&L = −0.167 × 2% = −0.33%.
d
One ordinary price move wipes out about 50 days of funding. This is the key fact behind Strategy C's results: the funding income is real but price moves dominate.

Step 4. Handling dead coins

A coin is tradable only on days with a price and positive volume. That removes LUNA after 2022-05-12 and FTT after 2022-11-14 (Binance's archive shows a frozen FTT price after that). A coin that stops trading between rebalances is closed out. Only 5 days missing from Binance's archive are forward-filled.

What drives the losses? (RQ5)

  • No market exposure: beta to daily BTC returns is −0.02 (t = −0.75). The strategy is market-neutral.
  • Losses are coin-specific. Total P&L by coin (percentage points): LUNA −87, XRP −58, DOGE −44, FTT −13; SOL +102, BNB +51, AVAX +36, ADA +32, LINK +27.
  • The LUNA week (ending 2022-05-15) lost 27.3%. The strategy was long LUNA: traders were shorting it, so its funding turned negative, and the rule puts lowest-funding coins in the long leg.
  • Other worst weeks: 2021-09-12 (−12.3%), 2022-11-13 FTX (−11.6%), 2020-11-29 (−11.0%), 2021-08-15 (−10.0%).
Mirror image of FX. FX carry crashes when crowded longs unwind. Crypto funding carry ends up long whatever traders short hardest, so it crashes when a heavily shorted coin collapses for real.

Implications. In both markets, carry looks like pay for bearing crash risk. In FX, positioning-based crowding is a cheap, modestly useful overlay. In crypto, funding measures leveraged demand, but sorting on it alone exposes you to single-coin collapses. A better crypto design would react faster than weekly, treat extreme negative funding as crowded shorts rather than a buy signal, and size positions by coin risk.

12Limitations

  • Sample start. Pre-euro DEXGEUS is missing, JPY rates start 2002 and CHF rates 1999, so Strategy A effectively starts in 1999-05.
  • Small crypto sample: about 6.5 years, 12 coins, 3–4 coins per leg, so single events dominate.
  • Crypto IS/OOS reversal (−0.07 vs 0.81). The out-of-sample period is short and driven by price, not funding.
  • Signal-horizon dependence: faster signals look better, but we keep the pre-specified one.
  • LRV data end in 2021-05, so the volatility regressions cover 1999–2021 only.
  • Simple costs: no slippage under stress, borrow constraints, funding caps or exchange counterparty risk (FTX shows this is real).
  • Only 33 crowded months, so the crowding effect is imprecise.

13Questions to expect

Click to reveal a suggested answer. Try answering out loud first.

Why can you compute carry returns without forward rates?
Covered interest parity: a forward position equals borrowing USD and depositing in the foreign currency. So the forward return is the foreign interest plus the spot change minus the US interest, which is our rx formula. CIP deviations exist (especially after 2008) but are small relative to monthly carry returns.
Why use CFTC data rather than something else for crowding?
It is the only long, public, weekly measure of speculative positions in currencies, going back to 1986. It directly measures who holds the trade, which is the mechanism in Brunnermeier, Nagel and Pedersen. Its limit: it covers only exchange futures, a small part of the FX market, so it is a proxy.
Why z-score positioning instead of using raw positions?
Normal positioning differs a lot by currency and changes over time (for example, speculators are structurally short JPY). The 36-month z-score asks "is this unusually crowded compared with recent history?", which is comparable across currencies.
The filter didn't avoid 2008. Doesn't that kill your hypothesis?
It shows crowding is not the only trigger. 2008 began from a non-crowded reading; the shock came from funding markets and volatility, which our regressions capture (vol beta −1.55). Crowding still predicts weaker next-month returns (0.02% vs 0.30%) and improves out-of-sample Sharpe in every variant of the grid. So it is a useful overlay, not a crash detector.
How do you know there's no look-ahead bias?
Weights formed at end of t earn t+1's return (shift by one period in code). CFTC Tuesday positions are used only after the Friday release (3-day lag). Crypto signals use funding through the previous day. Rolling z-scores use only past data.
Crypto out-of-sample Sharpe is 0.81. Isn't that great?
It's encouraging but we don't over-read it. It covers only about 2.7 years, and the decomposition shows most of the gain came from price moves (+13%), not funding (+3%). In-sample it was negative. We treat it as weak evidence, not a stable premium.
Why keep LUNA and FTT?
To avoid survivorship bias. Dropping coins that died would remove exactly the crashes we want to study. LUNA alone cost 87 percentage points of P&L.
Why was the strategy long LUNA when it crashed?
As LUNA fell, traders shorted the perp, pushing funding negative. The rule longs the lowest-funding coins (they pay you to be long). So the strategy bought what speculators were shorting hardest, right as it collapsed. It's the mirror image of FX carry's crash.
Why not switch to the 3-day daily version since it does better?
Choosing the best variant after seeing results is data snooping and would overstate expected performance. We fixed parameters in advance and report the grid for transparency. The pattern (faster is better) is a finding for future work, not a reason to change the headline.
Why does the combined portfolio have a lower Sharpe than A alone?
C had Sharpe 0.05 over 2021–2026, so mixing it in dilutes return. But volatility falls from about 9.4% to 7.0% and the worst month improves from −7.4% to −5.9%, because the correlation is only 0.11. With a better crypto leg, the diversification would raise the Sharpe.
How did you validate your FX implementation?
Correlation 0.82 with the LRV developed-country HML factor over 264 overlapping months, similar mean and Sharpe. Differences come from our smaller universe (7 currencies), 2-per-leg sort, and different rate data.
What would you do with more time?
Add pre-euro DEXGEUS to extend the FX sample; use VIX past 2021 for the volatility test; for crypto, use faster signals, treat extreme negative funding as a crowded-short warning, add coin-level risk sizing, and expand the coin universe.
